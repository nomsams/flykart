A fly brain in a four-wheel robot

This lab adds a metre-scale Three.js environment to FlyKart. It reuses the existing brain file importer, vision CNN and recurrent spiking controller. The pictured UNO, WiFi shield and motor driver are references for the legacy platform; the default target is an AI-Thinker-style ESP32-CAM controlling a separate L298N directly.

The photographs do not establish a complete pin map or identify the two missing wires. The default wiring below is a proposed simulation profile, not a reconstruction of the pictured build. The exact ESP32-CAM variant, motor-channel polarity, current under load, power supply and lens FOV need physical verification.

Wheel speed derives from tyre circumference × RPM / 60, scaled by effective motor voltage. At 100–240 RPM the unloaded kinematic range is about 0.34–0.82 m/s before driver losses. The quoted 20–48 m/min figures are consistent with that range. Each side's two motors share one H-bridge channel. Turning is differential skid steering with an editable slip factor, PWM start deadband and first-order motor lag. The simulation does not model full rigid-body dynamics, slopes, wheel suspension, electrical current, overheating, battery sag or torque over stones.

Contact uses a rotated rectangular chassis envelope, 26 × 17 cm and 10.5 cm high by default (sensor mount height plus a 4 cm payload allowance), with small movement substeps. Natural objects share their rendered convex meshes with collision: each mesh is clipped to chassis height before its contact footprint is tested. Empty corners around rounded bushes and rocks no longer block motion. Trees use their actual tapered trunks and any canopy that reaches chassis height. Tables, chairs and beds have separate legs and raised parts; the robot can pass underneath when the envelope clears the underside and fits between the legs. The selected object's bounding box indicates dimensions, not occupied collision space. Show contact shapes draws amber blocking footprints, blue drive-over surfaces and a green chassis envelope. These overlays are excluded from the sensor camera. Contact messages name the blocking part and never feed ground truth to the fly. The sonar retains its approximate box reflectors, separate from the detailed contact meshes. Water is a flat visual patch without depth or buoyancy physics.

Rooms, items and flooring

Choose Workshop room, Bedroom, Living room, Office, Wall maze, Clutter challenge, Garden or Empty floor. Add, move, rotate and resize individual blocks, shoes, beds, loose cables, doormats and the existing furniture and outdoor objects. Floor colour changes the actual rendered floor, so it also changes the camera pixels and the fly's visual input. Colour and all objects are preserved in browser storage and lab exports; older exports keep the original green floor.

Low cables and doormats are traversable when their height is at most 1.2 cm or 20% of wheel diameter, whichever is smaller. Crossing a cable reduces simulated ground speed by 25%; a doormat reduces it by 15%. Cables show a snag caution in the UI and console. Taller versions block the chassis. This is a simple traction approximation: there is no vertical wheel climbing, suspension, cable deformation or tangling model. Command odometry cannot observe the reduced traction. Blocks and shoes are solid obstacles. The default bed has 16 cm of clearance under its frame; its four legs still block the robot.

Camera and what the fly sees

The camera captures a rendered colour frame at 160 × 120, quantized to RGB565. This uses a practical QQVGA preset; it is not a claim that QQVGA is the sensor's smallest possible hardware window. Espressif documents RGB565 and small frame sizes for memory-constrained capture. The simulated horizontal field of view defaults to an assumed 66° and is editable; pixel format, FPS and optics do not establish actual performance of a particular module. See the Espressif camera driver and Espressif frame-size reference.

The displayed fly image is the actual centre crop resized to the imported vision network's dimensions (normally 48 × 24). That network estimates nine obstacle sectors and a surface value for world brains, or thirteen road / traffic cues for racing brains. No collision geometry, object IDs, exact distances or true pose enter the vision network. A world brain receives neutral mission inputs, so the lab does not impose a waypoint route. A racing brain retains its original input meanings instead of silently interpreting racing weights as world weights.

The bundled eyes were trained with the older software renderer and wider optics. Three.js lighting, furniture and real camera FOV introduce a substantial distribution shift. Successful transfer is unmeasured: a loaded brain can turn, stall or collide. The imported weights drive the robot; there is no hidden navigation expert. The Sonar avoidance example explicitly adds a hand-written reflex in the sketch. Training from Three.js / physical camera images and measuring held-out room performance are follow-up work.

HC-SR04

The nominal specification is 2–400 cm range with a quoted 15° measuring angle and a trigger pulse of at least 10 µs. The datasheet recommends more than 60 ms between measurements. The lab samples at about 66 ms and holds the last result, adds range noise and quantization, attenuates small or oblique surfaces, and accounts for target height. The displayed acoustic cone is an illustrative useful lobe, not a calibrated beam pattern. Source: HC-SR04 module datasheet.

Zero pulse width denotes a timeout, hence unknown distance. The example sketch returns −1 in that case. The fly's optional inputs 17 and 18 carry closeness and echo-valid, respectively. Existing robot brains used simulated echo strength at input 18; real HC-SR04 only provides pulse duration, so this lab substitutes echo-valid and labels the transfer as experimental. The simulated echo amplitude remains internal to detection and is not treated as a measured hardware output. Sonar cannot identify chairs, map a room by itself or reliably detect ground-level puddles.

Interactive pinout and wire diagram

Open Pinout & wires from the robot toolbar to follow controller GPIOs to the L298N, HC-SR04, onboard camera ribbon, two motor pairs and power rails. Select a wire or an entry in Connection list to inspect its source, destination, voltage level and purpose. The diagram shows the currently applied settings; Edit connections opens the component fields with a live draft preview. Changes take effect only after Apply components. Enable assignments of −1 appear as driver jumpers, not fictitious GPIO wires. Disconnected sensors use dashed links, invalid connections are marked red, and dots distinguish actual junctions from wire crossings. Save SVG exports a standalone vector diagram.

The pin reference lists the eight exposed spare GPIOs, UART / SD / flash sharing, camera-reserved pins and the AI-Thinker OV2640 signal mapping. The board is drawn as a logical breakout, not a physical header-orientation or FPC-contact drawing: match the labels printed on the actual module. The proposed 5 V regulator and power rails are illustrative; this simulator does not calculate electrical currents or power faults. The regulated rail assumes external 5 V logic power with the L298N module's onboard regulator jumper configured accordingly; that jumper is separate from the ENA/ENB jumpers. A passive ECHO-divider example uses 10 kΩ from ECHO to the input node and 15 kΩ from that node to GND, giving about 3.0 V from a 5 V echo. The legacy UNO view shows a direct L298N connection and an unspecified external camera bridge; it does not map the OSOYOO L293D/WiFi shield.

Camera pins are reserved using Espressif's AI-Thinker mapping; PSRAM pins are reserved too. Conflicts, duplicate GPIOs, missing ground, active SD sharing pins and unshifted ECHO inhibit virtual motors. GPIO12, 2 and 15 have boot-time constraints; particularly GPIO12 must not be held high at boot. GPIO4 shares the onboard flash circuit. Alternative use of GPIO1 / 3 conflicts with the programming UART. This pin budget is tight: an I/O expander or a different ESP32 camera board may be necessary for a robust physical build. The lab flags these constraints but does not simulate boot circuitry, pull resistors or flash LED electrical loads. See the official camera pin mappings.

The legacy UNO preset uses IN1–IN4 on D7–D10 and ENA/ENB on D5/D6. OSOYOO's V1.1 motor shield reference describes an L293DD driver. That shield and a separate L298N are different components; the lab models the latter, not both drivers in series. A UNO requires an external camera/brain bridge. In the ESP32 profile the UNO and shield are omitted.

Motor power and camera power need regulated supplies suited to their loads and a common reference ground. ESP32 GPIOs are logic only; do not power motors from them. Two motors in parallel per channel require stall-current measurements: the supplied 100–120 mA running-current figure cannot establish the required driver or supply headroom. L298 losses depend on current; consult ST's L298 datasheet and your module schematic.

Kenyon-inspired room memory

The 512-cell memory uses deterministic random projections of 24 coarse visual colour features. Only the sixteen most active cells learn per frame. Cells store running averages of observed scene estimates and visual prototypes. Recall requires enough previous observations and a close visual match; it happens before learning the current frame. A recalled estimate contributes 20% of the world controller's belief. It is a small engineered associative memory inspired by sparse Kenyon activity, not an anatomical fly connectome, biological learning model or proven SLAM system. Similar-looking views can alias.

The room map is separate: it marks observed sonar rays and uncertain echo endpoints using commanded-wheel dead reckoning. It contains no ground-truth object outlines and cannot tell where inside the beam a reflector lies. Slip and collisions cause drift. The initial pose is the known reset pose; there are no encoders, GPS or compass in this profile. Disable memory to run without its learned cues and mapping, or Forget room to clear it. Editing geometry or components clears learned memory to avoid stale layouts; resetting the robot retains it. Export lab preserves both neural associations and map observations.

Controller workbench and instruments

Choose Straight & turn check, Drive & scan, Sonar avoidance, Fly brain, Manual drive or My sketch. The editor shows the exact applied Arduino-style program; Apply code restarts its execution without resetting the room or chassis pose. Unsaved edits are highlighted by an unapplied status. Switching away from an edited preset keeps that text as My sketch during this session. Clear floor & run drive check prepares a reversible empty-floor diagnostic, resetting the robot and program clock. Run check in this habitat keeps the room and starts at the current pose. The sequence is a fixed drivetrain diagnostic, so contacts in a furnished room are reported rather than avoided. Explore with sonar selects the editable avoidance program; a single forward sonar cannot guarantee navigation around all objects. The repeating 17.2-second check drives forward, turns left twice with settling intervals, then repeats with right turns. The diagnostic measures distance, heading change and contacts; ground truth is used only to assess the check, not supplied to the fly. Four fixed wheels have no steering servo: “wheels straight” means stopping to let wheel speeds settle.

Autonomous modes continue when browser focus is lost. Manual mode pauses and releases keyboard commands on focus loss. There is no 27-second stop timer. The UI exposes pause reasons, obstacle contacts, drive/reverse/brake/steer outputs, motor requests and applied PWM. Arc steering scales turns by the drive request and prevents steering alone from spinning a stationary chassis. Pivot mode can be selected explicitly. L1/L2/L3 duplicate the left software request; R1/R2/R3 duplicate the right request. These selectable, invertible leg adapters are engineered labels, not identified anatomical leg neurons in the imported functional controller. Each L298N channel drives both wheels on its side.

The sensor console records held HC-SR04 echo measurements, camera frame statistics, Serial output, drive-check results and the neural inputs, visual estimates, outputs and motor requests. It retains at most 1,500 events and displays the latest 65 matching the selected filter. Export log includes the retained structured events; Save camera PNG exports the actual delivered 160 × 120 colour frame. Numeric noise controls provide camera Gaussian noise, exposure multiplier, black-frame dropouts, sonar distance jitter, echo dropouts and a repeatable seed. The displayed camera and fly crop derive from the same corrupted sensor frame. Sonar corruption is applied before pulseIn, controller inputs and room mapping. These are engineered noise models, not calibrated ESP32-CAM or HC-SR04 hardware error distributions. No added noise leaves the underlying acoustic model's uncertainty intact.

Editable code and saved experiments

The bounded interpreter parses numeric declarations, arithmetic, comparisons, if/else, helper functions, setup/loop and a small pin API. It never evaluates the sketch as JavaScript. Delays and pulseIn yield to the simulation scheduler, retaining statement position; writes affect virtual pins and therefore motor direction and PWM. Serial.print and Serial.println support strings and numbers. cameraFrame and cameraMeanR/G/B expose delivered frame statistics. Every loop is bounded, recursive calls are depth limited, and unsupported syntax reports an error. Numeric types are all floating point, so integer overflow / truncation, PWM carrier shape and CPU instruction timing are not emulated.

brainLeft() and brainRight() are simulation adapters. A downloaded .ino is a reviewable control sketch, not deployable complete ESP32 firmware: camera initialization, vision preprocessing, real inference or a WiFi brain bridge, and board-specific PWM timer allocation must be implemented. The neural weights and eyes currently execute in the browser; fitting or running this full pipeline on the ESP32-CAM has not been benchmarked. No hardware is flashed or driven by this page.

Export brain produces the existing FlyKart checkpoint format, retaining whether it has track or world semantics and 17 or 19 inputs. Import brain accepts original and combined vision brain files. Export lab stores the layout, components, wiring, editor text, controller, eyes and room memory in a versioned JSON file. Local browser storage keeps the habitat, component settings and applied sketch; it does not automatically preserve imported brains or learned cells. Export a lab to keep a full experiment. Object edits offer thirty levels of undo; import validates the complete file before replacing the session.
