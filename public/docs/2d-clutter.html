Vision lab · 3D habitat

Train navigation through clutter

Open world now includes seeded living room, workshop and bedroom layouts. Density changes the number of objects. “New clutter” keeps the room type and changes the seed. Export scene preserves the generated layout, including furniture clearance; importing older tree-and-rock scenes still works.

What the objects do now

  • Tables, chairs and beds: four legs block movement; the overhead surface blocks underneath only when its clearance is too low. Set clearance in centimetres under Clutter properties. The body is approximated by an 8-unit radius and 6.6 cm height. Camera and sonar use separate legs and raised tops. Choose the Robot sensor head for the low mounted camera.
  • Shoes: a contact can nudge them if there is room. Their camera geometry and sonar targets follow their new position. Gentle movable-object contacts have less impact pain than rigid contacts; an object trapped against another obstacle is harder to push. This is a simple displacement model, without measured mass or momentum.
  • Round blocks, trees and rocks: solid fixed obstacles. Blocks and shoes currently use circular bodies. Furniture tops use rectangles and legs use circles; clearance tests handle rectangle corners.
  • Mats and cables: visible floor clutter that reduces wheel speed inside its footprint. It does not create collision pain. Cable traction currently uses a circular influence area around the drawn cable; it is not a simulation of a cable wrapping around a wheel.

Impact pain uses the incoming speed squared, plus a small minimum contact cost. The contact weight slider scores accumulated pain while the console still reports raw contact count. Fatal impact budget is eight pain units. Ponds are still fatal. Sugar and pain score candidates; they do not reveal object locations to the neural controller.

Every training ghost receives its own copy of the scene and visual memory. Moving a shoe in one trial cannot clear the way for another candidate. Camera and sonar observations are generated from each candidate's current scene. The low resolution camera uses projected sprites, so this is preparation for the 3D world, not an identical renderer or verified physical simulation.

More memory and more looks

Kenyon visual recall now offers 40,000 cells in Open world and 3D. Virtual eye ensembles offer nine shifted crops as well as one, three, five and three zoom scales. Each crop has separate eye-network state, but all use the same physical camera image and trained eye weights. These are perception views; competing training ghosts do not share their beliefs. More cells do not increase the number of controller neurons. More crops and memory increase browser CPU and export size; neither guarantees better navigation.

The face guide explores different gazes, confidence voting and specialists trained on hard examples. The maze guide explores sparse visual familiarity, multiple scales, contrast filtering and keeping a sequence of place beliefs. Those results concern their own models and tasks. They are useful experiment ideas; their reported gains are not measurements of this robot.

Recommended next improvements

  1. A scene editor with object properties. Drag and rotate items; undo edits; specify mass, friction, softness, leg spacing and clearance per item. Save those recipes across 2D and 3D. Keep property labels out of neural inputs: the fly must infer them from its sensors.
  2. A mixed navigation curriculum. Start with empty forward and reverse practice, then floor hazards, light clutter, furniture passages, dead ends and moving obstacles. Always retain ordinary forward trials and reserve unseen room layouts for validation. A backing specialist should not replace a generalist on rear-goal scores alone.
  3. Specialists trained on failures. Save stalled, confused and high-impact episodes. Train new ensemble members more often on these cases, with a bounded sampling weight and ordinary journeys still included. Evaluate their combined vote on untouched tests. The current nine crops are not nine differently trained specialists.
  4. Sequence-based place memory. Maintain several possible locations from visual signatures and recent actions. Predict using measured odometry and correct with camera matches. Show ambiguity in repeated corridors. Add calibrated uncertainty and compare filtering methods; do not silently supply true simulator coordinates as a camera-only observation.
  5. Memory health and controlled forgetting. Show occupancy, novelty, ambiguous matches and recall age. Compare capacities and sparsity under equal compute budgets. Repeated views can saturate familiarity memory; increasing capacity alone does not solve this.
  6. Controlled perception comparisons. Benchmark one/three/five/nine views, scale crops, contrast normalization, smoothing and temporal averaging on identical held-out scenes. Measure arrival rate, impact pain, inference time and filtering delay. Calibrate confidence voting and align shifted spatial estimates before trusting an ensemble.
  7. Real-video appearance and material transfer. Photograph real furniture from the robot's low camera height. Match colour resolution, exposure, occlusion and motion blur. Randomize appearance separately from geometry, then validate recognition on recordings that were never used to teach it.
  8. Measured contact and clearance physics. Record SW-420 pulses, wheel slip, sonar returns beneath furniture and push tests on real objects. Fit the simulator to these observations. A vibration pulse or nearby sonar echo alone does not determine collision force; an unseen rear obstacle remains unknown to front-facing sensors.
